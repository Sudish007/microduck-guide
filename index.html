Field guide · October 2026

The $399 duck that walked onto NVIDIA's stage.

Microduck is a 25 cm, 800 g biped from Pollen Robotics, the Bordeaux team that is Hugging Face's robotics group. Every move it makes is a neural network trained in simulation. This guide covers why it is in the news this week, what it actually is, how to learn the whole stack for free, and what it honestly costs to build one from India.

How the software works

The robot's brain is pollen-robotics/microduck: a Rust workspace of small daemons talking JSON-RPC over Unix sockets. robotd owns the 50 Hz control loop and the servo bus. updaterd installs signed releases and rolls back if the robot boots unhealthy. mediad streams the camera over WebRTC, tofd serves the depth sensor, padd reads the gamepad, btd is the Bluetooth path for the phone app.

Behaviours come from microduck_rl: PPO on mjlab (MuJoCo Warp), trained at 50 Hz with a 61-number observation and a 14-number action. The sim-to-real recipe is the interesting part: a voltage-level actuator model (BAM) identified on a real servo bench, randomised battery voltage (6.5 to 8.2 V), randomised command delay (3 to 6 control steps) and an explicit backlash hinge in series with every joint. Policies export to ONNX and the runtime hot-swaps them (walk, recover, trick) behind the shared observation contract.

# train the walking policy (CUDA GPU, ~1-2 h for a usable gait at 4096 envs)
uv run train Mjlab-Velocity-Flat-MicroDuck --env.scene.num-envs 4096

# no GPU? run the same job on Hugging Face Jobs
uv run train Mjlab-Velocity-Flat-MicroDuck --hf-jobs

# export to ONNX, then drive it in CPU MuJoCo with the keyboard
uv run scripts/export.py Mjlab-Velocity-Flat-MicroDuck --wandb-run-path <...>
uv run scripts/infer_policy.py --walking output.onnx

The seven shipped moves

Walk (velocity tracking) · Sit & stand · Kick · Grab (beak to floor) · Roller skating (with skates) · Get back up, plus crouching and a forward roll (“roulade”) depending on how Pollen counts. Ten policy files on the Hub cover these and the roller variants.

What the community has inferred inferred

  • Servos are almost certainly Dynamixel XL330-M288-T: the training code loads the BAM “m6” model that Rhoban identified on that exact part, and the public mesh is referenced 15 times. Pollen has never named the sub-model. Evidence chain.
  • The servos run at 6.6 to 8.2 V from the 2S battery, above the XL330's 6.0 V rating. The open HAT schematic shows no regulator between battery and servo connectors. More torque, more heat. Copy it and you take on the same trade-off.
  • The compute board is RK3566-class (Radxa Zero 3W is the usual guess from the teardowns); Pollen states only the SoC.

03 · Exactly how open

Open-source software. Not open-source hardware.

This distinction decides whether you can “build the exact same thing”. Pollen's own press kit asks journalists not to call it open-source hardware. Here is the line.

open · Apache-2.0

  • Robot runtime and all daemons: pollen-robotics/microduck, with design docs and an open roadmap.
  • Training stack: microduck_rl, including the BAM actuator model, domain randomisation and the reward-design playbook (AGENTS.md).
  • Every shipped policy: microduck-policies on the Hub, retrainable and replaceable with robotctl policy add.
  • The in-browser simulator (Sandbox), the Arena leaderboard, the duck detector and its training pipeline.
  • The MuJoCo model (MJCF) and the STL meshes of every part are public, so the simulation twin is complete.
  • The Raspberry Pi robot HAT from the prototype era (elec_RPI_Robot_HAT) is published with a KiCad schematic, per the community teardowns; it is how the servo over-voltage was confirmed.

not released

  • No official bill of materials, no editable CAD, no production PCB files.
  • The 3D model is public for simulation, but community projects report it carries a non-commercial licence. Printing a copy for yourself is the use case; selling kits is not.
  • No official statement of the servo part number, the compute board or the battery electronics beyond the SoC and NP-F550.
  • Spare parts come through the $119 Dev pack rather than an open supply chain.

Consequence: an exact Microduck can only be approximated by a replica built from the public meshes plus community reverse-engineering. The fully open-hardware duck is its predecessor, Open Duck Mini v2, by the same engineer, with a published BOM, Onshape CAD, print guide and runtime.

05 · Build routes

Four ways to get a duck. Only one is cheap.

The uncomfortable fact first: fifteen Dynamixel XL330s cost more than the whole $399 robot. Pollen's price is a volume price. Below are the routes ranked by how close they get to “the exact same thing”, with the honest cost of each.

ROUTE A · EXACT

Buy the Microduck

$399+ $39 charger pack recommended · before tax, shipping, duty

The only way to get the real hardware, the NPU, the ToF sensor, the NFC and the signed update channel.

  • Launch markets: North America, Europe, UK. India is not listed yet.
  • Pre-order now, delivery targeted Dec 2026.
  • Spares only via the $119 Dev pack.
fact Cheapest path to the exact robot, if and when it ships to India or you have a forwarding address.
ROUTE B · CLOSEST REPLICA

XL330 replica

₹60k to 1.2Lparts, prints, tools, 10% spares · servos are two-thirds of it

Same servo family, same joint layout, printed from the public meshes. One build (AI-FanGe) walks on video. No official policy runs unmodified; the public ONNX files were trained for Pollen's exact mass and IMU placement, so expect to retrain or at least re-check offsets.

  • 14 to 15× Dynamixel XL330-M288-T (import).
  • Pi Zero 2 W + OpenRB-150, or Radxa Zero 3W to match the original.
  • BNO085 IMU, 2S pack, 5 V buck, PLA prints.
estimate For people who want the Microduck form factor specifically and accept paying more than retail.
ROUTE C · OPEN HARDWARE

Open Duck Mini v2

₹38k to 72keverything buyable in India · ₹38k if you own a printer, controller and tools

The predecessor by the same engineer, fully open: BOM, Onshape CAD, print guide, assembly guide, pre-built Pi image and two walking policies. Bigger (42 cm, 2.1 kg) because it uses the heavier, cheaper Feetech STS3215.

  • 14× Feetech STS3215 (stocked by Indian shops).
  • Pi Zero 2 W, BNO055, USB-serial bus adapter, LiPo.
  • An India-sourced build (Jero) exists.
estimate Our pick for a real build. Same RL recipe, same community, legal to share and sell parts for.
ROUTE D · ZERO RUPEES

Simulation only

₹0plus optional ₹100 to 500 of cloud GPU

Everything in Levels 0 to 3 of the learning path. You can train, evaluate, publish and race policies on the official Arena without owning a robot. It is the same workflow the shipped policies were made with: simulation first, transfer second.

  • Browser sandbox, CPU MuJoCo, Colab or HF Jobs.
  • Publish to the Hub; install on any owner's duck later.
  • Zero hardware risk, zero waiting.
fact Where everyone should start. Buy hardware only after a policy you trained walks in sim.

Two safety notes before any build. The original runs its XL330s above their rated 6.0 V; replicas that copy the 2S battery do too. Budget for a spare servo or two, keep an eye on heat, and never disable servo protections to “fix” a brown-out. And treat lithium packs properly: a 2S Li-ion/LiPo with a real charger and a switch on the positive lead, never a bare pack feeding a Pi directly.

The build sequence (both DIY routes)

Condensed from the Open Duck Mini assembly guide and the OpenELAB / AI-FanGe Microduck replica write-up. Each step is a checkpoint; do not move on until it passes.

  1. Simulate first, buy second

    Run the official policies in CPU MuJoCo and train one gait yourself (Level 3). You will understand joint order, offsets and the observation contract before a single servo arrives.

  2. Order parts in two batches

    Batch one: one servo, the bus adapter, the IMU, the Pi. Prove the bus and the IMU on your desk. Batch two: the remaining servos, battery, printing. This caps your loss if a part choice is wrong.

  3. Print the structure

    PLA for structure, TPU where the design calls for it (Open Duck feet). Check servo cavities with one real servo before printing the full set. Replicas should use the FDM-redesigned parts (BoBoRedesign), not the raw simulation meshes.

  4. Configure every servo alone

    One at a time: set a unique ID, 1 Mbps baud, return delay 0, centre the horn, label it. Dynamixel Wizard 2.0 for XL330; the Feetech tool or the Open Duck scripts for STS3215. Fourteen factory-default servos on one bus cannot be told apart later.

  5. Assemble legs, then body, then head

    Feet up. Finish both legs before joining them to the body; install the recessed screws before the next servo covers them; keep the head open until the neck servos and (optionally) the beak are secured. Threadlocker on metal-to-metal screws.

  6. Wire power in two branches

    Switched battery positive splits to the servo bus directly and to a 5 V buck for the Pi. Three daisy-chains (right leg, left leg, head/neck) joined supply-to-supply, ground-to-ground, data-to-data. IMU on I²C, mounted square to the body frame, orientation matching the config.

  7. Flash the image, find it on Wi-Fi

    Both projects publish a ready Pi image. 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi only on the Pi Zero 2 W. SSH in, change the default password, read servo voltages and IMU before anything moves.

  8. Calibrate, then a supported first stand

    Zero-pose and knee offsets must match your assembly, not the tutorial's. Hold the robot, enable torque, check the neutral pose, then stand it on a clear floor with a hand ready. Walking comes last and starts at zero velocity.

  9. Keep a vet book

    Log hours, falls and every servo swap, the way the real robot's robotctl vetbook does. The data tells you when a servo is dying before it strips a gear mid-walk.

The low-budget ladder

If money is tight, climb this in order. Each rung is worth doing on its own and de-risks the next.

RUNG 0 · ₹0
Simulation

Browser sandbox, CPU MuJoCo, Colab. Train and publish a policy. Enter the Arena sprint.

Stop here until a gait you trained walks in sim.

RUNG 1 · ₹4k to 7k
One-servo bench

One STS3215 (₹1.9k to 2.3k) or one XL330 (import), a USB bus adapter (₹600 to 1.2k), a printed pendulum arm and weights.

Reproduce BAM's actuator identification. You now understand the sim-to-real gap physically.

RUNG 2 · ₹12k to 18k
One leg + IMU

Five servos, the Pi Zero 2 W, the IMU, a bench supply. Drive one leg from a policy's joint targets; log the tracking error.

Parts carry straight into the full build.

RUNG 3 · ₹38k to 72k
Open Duck Mini v2

Add nine servos, battery, prints, hardware. A walking open-hardware biped, all parts sourced in India.

Second-hand printer or a print service keeps this at the low end.

RUNG 4 · $399 + duty
Real Microduck

When India is a shipping destination, or via a forwarding address. The NPU, ToF, NFC and signed updates are not replicable at this price.

By then your policies already run in its simulator.

Training compute is not a hardware cost. You do not need a GPU in the house. A walking policy on Hugging Face Jobs is about an hour on an L4 at roughly $0.80 (under ₹100). Google Colab's free tier works through the Colab lab port. A CPU-only trainer (mjbatch) reached a stable walk in 2 h 36 min on 8 vCPU. Buy a GPU for robotics only when you are iterating many times a day.
